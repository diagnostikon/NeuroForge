────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
01. OVERVIEW & BASIC FACTS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The human brain is the most complex known object in the universe — a roughly
1.4-kilogram mass of electrochemical machinery that generates every thought,
emotion, perception, memory, and voluntary action a human being ever has.

QUICK STATS:
  Weight               ~1,300 – 1,400 grams (average adult)
  Volume               ~1,130 cm³ (female avg) / ~1,260 cm³ (male avg)
  Neurons              ~86 billion (±8 billion)
  Non-neuronal cells   ~85 billion (glial, endothelial, etc.)
  Synapses             ~100–500 TRILLION (estimates vary; ~150T is commonly cited)
  Cortical surface     ~2,500 cm² (if unfolded; ~2.5 sq ft)
  Cortical thickness   ~2–4 mm
  White matter length  ~170,000 km of axon fibers (if stretched end to end)
  Power consumption    ~20 watts (the brain uses 20% of body's energy, ~20W)
  Processing speed     ~120 m/s (fastest nerve impulses)
  Metabolic demand     Glucose is primary fuel; ~5.6 mg glucose/100g brain/min
  Operating temp       ~38.5°C (slightly warmer than body core)
  Age at peak          Prefrontal cortex fully matures at ~25 years
  Lifespan             Neurons can survive 100+ years; brain ages over lifetime

The brain is housed inside the skull (cranium) and is cushioned by three
membranes called meninges: dura mater, arachnoid mater, and pia mater.
Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulates between these layers, acting as a hydraulic
shock absorber and metabolic clearance system. The blood-brain barrier (BBB),
formed by tight junctions in cerebrovascular endothelial cells, selectively
controls what enters the brain from the bloodstream — keeping out pathogens and
most large molecules while allowing glucose, oxygen, and small lipophilic
molecules through.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
02. EVOLUTIONARY HISTORY
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The brain did not spring into existence fully formed. It is the product of
~500 million years of iterative evolutionary engineering.

  ~540 MYA   Primitive nervous systems in early bilaterians (nerve nets)
  ~500 MYA   First centralized ganglia in ancient fish ancestors
  ~420 MYA   Hindbrain / brainstem structures emerge (survival, reflexes)
  ~350 MYA   Limbic precursors in reptilian ancestors (emotion, memory)
  ~200 MYA   Early mammalian neocortex begins to form
  ~65 MYA    Primate brain expansion begins (visual cortex enlargement)
  ~6-7 MYA   Hominid lineage diverges from chimpanzees; brain ~350cc
  ~3 MYA     Homo habilis; brain ~600cc; tool use begins
  ~2 MYA     Homo erectus; brain ~900cc; fire, social coordination
  ~300 KYA   Homo sapiens; brain ~1,350cc; language, abstract thought
  ~70 KYA    Cognitive revolution — symbolic thought, art, complex language

MacLean's "Triune Brain" model (now considered oversimplified but pedagogically
useful) divides the brain into:
  - Reptilian complex (brainstem + basal ganglia): survival instincts
  - Paleomammalian complex (limbic system): emotions, memory
  - Neomammalian complex (neocortex): reasoning, language, planning

Modern neuroscience rejects strict triune modularity but the evolutionary
layering is real — older brain structures are preserved and co-opted.

03. PHYSICAL COMPOSITION
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

BY WEIGHT:
  Water              ~73–78%
  Lipids (fats)      ~11% (highest fat content of any organ; myelin is mostly fat)
  Proteins           ~8%
  Carbohydrates      ~1%
  Inorganic salts    ~1%
  Other              trace minerals, RNA, DNA

STRUCTURAL DIVISIONS:
  Cerebrum           Largest part; two hemispheres; seat of higher cognition
  Cerebellum         Posterior; balance, motor coordination, procedural learning
  Brainstem          Midbrain + pons + medulla; vital autonomic functions
  Diencephalon       Thalamus + hypothalamus; sensory relay, homeostasis
  Limbic System      Hippocampus, amygdala, cingulate, etc.; emotion & memory

GRAY MATTER vs. WHITE MATTER:
  Gray matter  → Cell bodies, dendrites, unmyelinated axons, synapses
                 Location: cortex (outer layer), subcortical nuclei
                 Function: information processing, computation
  White matter → Myelinated axon tracts (highways between regions)
                 Location: beneath cortex, corpus callosum, brainstem
                  Function: signal transmission, connectivity

                  (See Section 04 for cellular details.)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
04. CELLULAR ARCHITECTURE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

THE NEURON (nerve cell) — the fundamental computational unit:

  Structure:
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  DENDRITES ──► CELL BODY (soma) ──► AXON ──► AXON TERMINAL │
  │      ↑                                            ↓         │
  │  Receive signals                          Release signals   │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  Components:
  - Dendrites: branching input receivers; one neuron may have thousands
  - Soma (cell body): contains nucleus, DNA, protein synthesis machinery
  - Axon hillock: decision point — fires or doesn't fire (all-or-nothing)
  - Axon: single long output cable; ranges from 0.1mm to 1+ meter (spinal)
  - Myelin sheath: fatty insulation around axon; speeds signal ~100x
  - Nodes of Ranvier: gaps in myelin where signal regenerates (saltatory)
  - Axon terminals (boutons): release neurotransmitters into synaptic cleft
  - Synaptic vesicles: membrane sacs containing neurotransmitter molecules

TYPES OF NEURONS:
  Sensory (afferent)   → Carry signals FROM body TO brain
  Motor (efferent)     → Carry signals FROM brain TO muscles/glands
  Interneurons         → Connect neurons within CNS (~99% of all neurons)

  Morphological types:
  - Pyramidal neurons (cortex: excitatory, glutamatergic)
  - Purkinje cells (cerebellum: massive dendritic trees, inhibitory)
  - Granule cells (smallest; most numerous; cerebellum & olfactory bulb)
  - Bipolar neurons (sensory organs)
  - Mirror neurons (action-observation; motor cortex, Broca's area)
  - Von Economo neurons (empathy, social awareness; frontoinsular cortex)

GLIAL CELLS (~85 billion) — not just "support" cells:
  Astrocytes      Regulate synapses, BBB maintenance, metabolic support,
                  neurotransmitter recycling, synchronize neural activity
  Oligodendrocytes Form myelin in CNS (one cell myelinates ~50 axons)
  Schwann cells   Form myelin in PNS (one cell per axon segment)
  Microglia       Immune cells of the brain; prune excess synapses,
                  phagocytose debris, respond to injury
  Ependymal cells Line ventricles; produce and circulate CSF
  Radial glia     Guide neuron migration during development

05. BRAIN REGIONS & THEIR FUNCTIONS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The brain is divided into regions with specialized (but heavily interconnected)
functions. The mapping below is a high-level functional atlas.

CEREBRAL CORTEX — The 6-layered outer mantle of the brain:

  Layer I   (Molecular layer)    — Mostly dendrites and axons; few cells
  Layer II  (External granular)  — Small pyramidal and stellate cells
  Layer III (External pyramidal) — Medium pyramidal cells; cortico-cortical
  Layer IV  (Internal granular)  — Receives thalamic sensory input
  Layer V   (Internal pyramidal) — Large Betz cells; output to spinal cord
  Layer VI  (Multiform layer)    — Projects back to thalamus

Cortical organization:
  - Brodmann Areas: 52 cytoarchitecturally distinct regions (Korbinian
    Brodmann, 1909) — still used as anatomical reference
  - Functional columns: vertical units ~0.5mm wide; basic processing modules

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
07. SUBCORTICAL STRUCTURES
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

THALAMUS:
  - Gateway / relay station for almost all sensory inputs to cortex
  - Exceptions: olfaction (goes directly to cortex)
  - Nuclei: ~50+ distinct nuclei with specific projection targets
  - Role in consciousness: thalamocortical loops are critical
  - Mediodorsal nucleus → prefrontal (cognition)
  - Pulvinar → parietal/temporal/occipital (attention, visual integration)
  - Lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) → V1 (vision)
  - Medial geniculate nucleus (MGN) → A1 (hearing)

HYPOTHALAMUS:
  - Master regulator of homeostasis
  - Controls: hunger, thirst, temperature, sleep-wake, circadian rhythm
  - Controls pituitary gland → hormonal regulation of whole body
  - Nuclei: suprachiasmatic (circadian), arcuate (appetite), paraventricular,
    lateral (hunger), ventromedial (satiety), preoptic (thermoregulation)
  - Releases: CRH, GnRH, TRH, GHRH, somatostatin, oxytocin, vasopressin

BASAL GANGLIA (striatum + globus pallidus + subthalamic + substantia nigra):
  - Action selection: which behaviors to execute or suppress
  - Habit formation and procedural learning
  - Reward-based learning (dopamine from substantia nigra pars compacta)
  - Direct pathway: facilitates movement
  - Indirect pathway: suppresses competing movements
  - Dysfunction: Parkinson's (dopamine loss), Huntington's (striatum),
                  OCD, addiction, Tourette syndrome

AMYGDALA:
  - Emotional processing hub (especially fear and threat detection)
  - Modulates memory consolidation (emotional events remembered better)
  - Social threat detection; eye contact; face emotional expressions
  - Basolateral amygdala (BLA): fear learning, associative
  - Central amygdala: fear expression, autonomic output
  - Basal amygdala: reward, motivation

HIPPOCAMPUS:
  - Critical for declarative (explicit) memory encoding
  - Spatial navigation (place cells, grid cells)
  - Pattern separation and completion
  - CA1, CA3, DG (dentate gyrus) subfields — distinct computations
  - One of few brain areas with adult neurogenesis (still debated)
  - HM (Henry Molaison): bilateral hippocampectomy → anterograde amnesia;
    proved hippocampus essential for long-term memory formation

NUCLEUS ACCUMBENS (NAc):
  - Core of the brain's reward circuit
  - Dopamine flood here = pleasure/reward signal
  - Involved in: pleasure, reinforcement learning, addiction, motivation
  - Gateway between limbic emotion and motor action ("wanting" system)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
08. THE BRAINSTEM
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The brainstem (midbrain + pons + medulla oblongata) is the most ancient and
life-critical part of the brain. Brain death = brainstem death.

MIDBRAIN (mesencephalon):
  Superior colliculus    → Visual reflexes, eye movements, gaze control
  Inferior colliculus    → Auditory reflexes, sound localization
  Periaqueductal gray    → Pain modulation, fear, defensive behavior
  Substantia nigra       → Dopamine production; motor control
  Red nucleus            → Motor coordination (with cerebellum)
  Reticular formation    → Arousal, consciousness, sleep-wake

PONS:
  - Relay between cerebellum and cortex
  - Cranial nuclei: V (trigeminal), VI (abducens), VII (facial), VIII (vestibulocochlear)
  - Respiratory rhythm control (pontine respiratory group)
  - REM sleep generation (locus coeruleus, raphe nuclei)
  - Locus coeruleus: norepinephrine; alertness, stress response
  - Raphe nuclei: serotonin; mood, arousal, pain

MEDULLA OBLONGATA:
  - Most vital brainstem structure; damage = instant death
  - Cardiovascular control center (heart rate, blood pressure)
  - Respiratory center (breathing rhythm)
  - Vomiting, swallowing, coughing, sneezing centers
  - Cranial nuclei: IX (glossopharyngeal), X (vagus), XI, XII
  - Pyramidal decussation: motor pathways cross here (left brain → right body)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
09. THE CEREBELLUM
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The cerebellum ("little brain") contains MORE neurons than the rest of the
brain combined (~69 billion, mostly granule cells).

STRUCTURE:
  - Two hemispheres + central vermis
  - Outer cerebellar cortex (gray matter)
  - Deep cerebellar nuclei (dentate, interpositus, fastigial)

FUNCTION:
  - Motor coordination: smoothing and timing of voluntary movements
  - Balance and posture
  - Procedural learning (learning to ride a bike)
  - Timing and rhythm
  - Predictive modeling: internal forward models of movement
  - Increasingly recognized: role in cognition, language, emotion

COMPUTATIONAL PRINCIPLE:
  - Receives "corollary discharge" copies of motor commands
  - Compares predicted vs. actual sensory feedback
  - Corrects errors in real-time and updates internal models
  - Classic supervised learning in biology

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
10. THE LIMBIC SYSTEM
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The limbic system is a set of interconnected structures mediating emotion,
memory, motivation, and survival behavior. Key players:

  Hippocampus         → Memory encoding (episodic/spatial)
  Amygdala            → Emotional tagging, fear, threat detection
  Hypothalamus        → Homeostasis, drives (hunger, sex, aggression)
  Cingulate Cortex:
    Anterior (ACC)    → Error detection, conflict monitoring, pain affect
    Posterior (PCC)   → Self-referential thought, mind-wandering (DMN hub)
  Entorhinal Cortex   → Memory consolidation gateway to hippocampus
  Parahippocampal Ctx → Scene/context memory
  Fornix              → Major output tract from hippocampus
  Mammillary Bodies   → Memory (Papez circuit); damaged in Korsakoff syndrome
  Olfactory Bulb      → Only sense with direct limbic access (emotion-smell link)

PAPEZ CIRCUIT (James Papez, 1937):
  Hippocampus → Fornix → Mammillary Bodies → Anterior Thalamus →
  Cingulate Cortex → Entorhinal Cortex → Hippocampus

  This loop is foundational to emotional memory processing.
  (See Section 17 for memory systems and Section 19 for emotion.)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
11. THE CONNECTOME — WIRING OF THE BRAIN
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The connectome is the complete map of all neural connections in a nervous system.

LEVELS OF CONNECTIVITY:
  Macro-connectome    → Large-scale white matter tracts (MRI tractography)
  Meso-connectome     → Connectivity between cortical areas at column level
  Micro-connectome    → Synapse-by-synapse (electron microscopy)

MAJOR WHITE MATTER TRACTS:
  Corpus Callosum     → Largest; connects left and right hemispheres
  Arcuate Fasciculus  → Broca's ↔ Wernicke's; language pathway
  Uncinate Fasciculus → Frontal ↔ temporal; emotion-memory-behavior
  Superior Longitudinal Fasciculus → Parietal ↔ frontal; spatial attention
  Corticospinal Tract → Motor cortex → spinal cord (voluntary movement)
  Optic Radiations    → LGN → V1 (visual signals)
  Fornix              → Hippocampus → hypothalamus/thalamus

DEFAULT MODE NETWORK (DMN):
  - Active during rest, self-referential thought, daydreaming
  - Nodes: mPFC, PCC, angular gyrus, hippocampus, lateral temporal
  - Deactivated during focused external tasks
  - Implicated in episodic memory retrieval, future simulation, theory of mind
  - Disrupted in Alzheimer's, depression, schizophrenia

MAJOR FUNCTIONAL NETWORKS:
  Salience Network    → Anterior insula + ACC; detects important stimuli
  Executive Network   → dlPFC + parietal; goal-directed cognition
  Sensorimotor        → Motor + somatosensory cortices; movement/touch
  Visual Network      → Occipital areas; visual processing
  Auditory Network    → Superior temporal; sound processing
  Limbic Network      → Amygdala + hippocampus + orbitofrontal; emotion

12. NEURONS & SYNAPSES
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

HOW A NEURON FIRES:

  Resting potential:
    - Neuron interior: ~-70 mV relative to outside
    - Maintained by Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in per cycle)
    - K⁺ leaks out; Na⁺ mostly blocked at rest
    - Chloride (Cl⁻) ions mostly outside

  Graded potentials:
    - Subthreshold changes in membrane voltage from synaptic input
    - Excitatory Post-Synaptic Potential (EPSP): depolarization (+)
    - Inhibitory Post-Synaptic Potential (IPSP): hyperpolarization (-)
    - Summation: spatial (multiple inputs at once) + temporal (rapid repeated)

  Action potential (spike):
    1. Threshold reached (~-55 mV): voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open
    2. Rapid depolarization to ~+40 mV (Na⁺ rushes in)
    3. Repolarization: Na⁺ channels inactivate, K⁺ channels open
    4. Hyperpolarization: K⁺ channels slow to close (-90 mV)
    5. Refractory period: absolute (~1-2ms) then relative
    6. Returns to resting potential

THE SYNAPSE — the communication junction:

  Types:
    Chemical synapse  → Most common; neurotransmitter-mediated; 20-40nm cleft
    Electrical synapse → Gap junctions; ions flow directly; fast; synchrony

  Chemical synapse process:
    1. Action potential arrives at axon terminal
    2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ enters terminal
    3. Vesicles dock and fuse with presynaptic membrane (SNARE proteins)
    4. Neurotransmitters released into synaptic cleft (~5000 molecules/vesicle)
    5. Neurotransmitters bind to receptors on postsynaptic membrane
    6. Ion channels open (ionotropic) or G-protein cascade (metabotropic)
    7. Effect: EPSP or IPSP on postsynaptic neuron
    8. Termination: reuptake, enzymatic degradation, diffusion

SYNAPTIC PLASTICITY:
  Long-Term Potentiation (LTP):
    - Repeated co-activation strengthens synapse
    - "Neurons that fire together wire together" (Hebb's rule)
    - NMDA receptor acts as coincidence detector (requires both pre+post active)
    - Results in: more AMPA receptors inserted, structural spine growth
    - Molecular basis of learning and memory

  Long-Term Depression (LTD):
    - Persistent decrease in synaptic strength
    - Occurs when postsynaptic fires without presynaptic (or low-freq stim)
    - Important for erasure, selectivity, cerebellum motor learning

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
13. ELECTROCHEMICAL SIGNALING
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

SIGNAL PROPAGATION:
  Unmyelinated axon: continuous wave of depolarization; ~0.5-2 m/s
  Myelinated axon:   saltatory conduction (node to node); ~70-120 m/s

INFORMATION CODING:
  Rate coding:       Firing rate encodes signal intensity (20-200 Hz typical)
  Temporal coding:   Precise spike timing encodes information
  Population coding: Distributed pattern across many neurons encodes meaning
  Sparse coding:     Small fraction of neurons active at once (energy efficient)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
14. NEUROTRANSMITTERS & NEUROMODULATORS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MAJOR NEUROTRANSMITTERS:

  Glutamate (GLU):
    - Primary excitatory NT in CNS (~90% of synapses are glutamatergic)
    - Receptors: AMPA (fast), NMDA (coincidence detector), kainate, mGluR
    - Role: learning, memory, sensation, motor control

  GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid):
    - Primary inhibitory NT in CNS (~20% of neurons are GABAergic)
    - Receptors: GABA-A (fast Cl⁻ channel), GABA-B (slow; G-protein)
    - Role: preventing overexcitation, anxiety reduction, sleep
    - Drugs: benzodiazepines, alcohol, barbiturates → potentiate GABA-A

  Glycine:
    - Inhibitory; especially in brainstem and spinal cord
    - Receptor: GlyR (Cl⁻ channel)

NEUROMODULATORS (broadcast systems):

  Dopamine (DA):
    - Produced: substantia nigra (motor), ventral tegmental area (VTA, reward)
    - Pathways: nigrostriatal, mesolimbic, mesocortical, tuberoinfundibular
    - Encodes: reward prediction error (RPE) — not just pleasure
    - Role: motivation, salience, reinforcement learning, motor control
    - Deficit: Parkinson's (motor), anhedonia (mood)
    - Excess: schizophrenia (positive symptoms); hyperkinesia

  Serotonin (5-HT):
    - Produced: raphe nuclei (brainstem); gut (~90% of body's serotonin)
    - 14+ receptor subtypes
    - Role: mood regulation, appetite, sleep, social behavior, nausea
    - Low serotonin: depression, OCD, aggression
    - SSRIs block reuptake → more 5-HT in synapse → antidepressant effect

  Norepinephrine (NE):
    - Produced: locus coeruleus (pons)
    - Role: arousal, alertness, fight-or-flight, attention, memory
    - Floods during stress; primes brain for rapid response
    - Role in PTSD, ADHD; NE reuptake inhibitors = stimulants/antidepressants

  Acetylcholine (ACh):
    - Produced: basal forebrain (cognitive), brainstem (REM sleep)
    - Receptors: nicotinic (ionotropic), muscarinic (metabotropic)
    - Role: learning, attention, REM sleep, muscle activation (NMJ)
    - Deficit: Alzheimer's disease (basal forebrain degeneration)
    - Organophosphate poisons: block acetylcholinesterase → paralysis

  Endorphins / Enkephalins / Dynorphins (opioid peptides):
    - Pain modulation, euphoria, stress response
    - Bind to µ, δ, κ opioid receptors
    - "Runner's high" = endorphin + endocannabinoid release

  Endocannabinoids (eCBs):
    - Retrograde signaling: released from postsynaptic, act on presynaptic
    - Suppress neurotransmitter release (inhibit inhibition or excitation)
    - Role: pain, appetite, memory, mood, neuroprotection
    - CB1 receptors (brain), CB2 receptors (immune + periphery)
    - THC (cannabis) mimics anandamide at CB1

  Oxytocin:
    - Produced: hypothalamus (paraventricular nucleus)
    - Released: pituitary gland into bloodstream + directly into brain
    - Role: social bonding, trust, maternal behavior, orgasm, childbirth
    - "Bonding hormone" — increased by touch, eye contact, social warmth

  Vasopressin (ADH):
    - Hypothalamus → pituitary
    - Role: water retention, blood pressure, social behavior, aggression
    - V1a receptor: social memory, partner bonding (monogamy in voles)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
15. NEURAL OSCILLATIONS & BRAINWAVES
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The brain generates rhythmic electrical activity at various frequencies.
Each frequency band correlates with specific cognitive/behavioral states.

  Delta (δ)    0.5–4 Hz     Deep sleep (NREM stages 3–4); unconsciousness
  Theta (θ)    4–8 Hz       Drowsiness, meditation, REM, hippocampal memory
                             encoding, creative insight, spatial navigation
  Alpha (α)    8–13 Hz      Relaxed wakefulness, eyes closed; inhibition of
                             task-irrelevant areas; "idling" rhythm
  Beta (β)     13–30 Hz     Active thinking, problem-solving, alertness,
                             motor activity; sensorimotor rhythm ~12-15 Hz
  Gamma (γ)    30–100 Hz    High cognitive processing, feature binding,
                             conscious perception, working memory, attention
  High-Gamma   100–200 Hz   Local cortical activity; closely tracks neural
                             firing; used in BCI decoding

KEY OSCILLATORY PHENOMENA:
  Phase-locking:    Different brain regions synchronize oscillations
  Theta-gamma coupling: Theta organizes gamma "packets" → memory encoding
  Phase precession: Hippocampal place cells fire earlier each theta cycle
  Neural avalanches: Scale-free cascades; brain at criticality
  Slow cortical potentials: Sustained shifts in cortical excitability

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
16. NEUROPLASTICITY
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and function
in response to experience, learning, or injury.

TYPES:
  Synaptic plasticity   LTP/LTD; strength changes at existing synapses
  Structural plasticity Spine growth/retraction; new synapse formation
  Axonal remodeling     Sprouting, pruning, path changes
  Myelination changes   Experience alters white matter structure
  Neurogenesis          New neurons in adult hippocampus (DG) and olfactory
  Cortical remapping    Deprived cortex colonized by adjacent areas

CRITICAL PERIODS:
  - Windows of heightened plasticity during development
  - Visual cortex: 4 months–4 years (ocular dominance columns)
  - Language: birth–puberty
  - Closing mechanism: PV interneuron maturation, PNN (perineuronal net) formation
  - Can be reopened by: ketamine, dark exposure (vision), enriched environment

ADULT PLASTICITY:
  - Still occurs but slower and less global
  - Learning London map → hippocampal volume increase (London cab drivers)
  - Musicians: enlarged auditory, somatosensory, motor cortex
  - Meditation: increased cortical thickness in insula and PFC
   - Exercise: promotes BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) → neurogenesis
   (See Section 17 for memory and consolidation, Section 11 for connectome.)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
17. MEMORY SYSTEMS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Memory is not a single thing — it is a collection of distinct systems.

EXPLICIT (DECLARATIVE) MEMORY — conscious recall:
  Episodic memory:
    - Personal autobiographical events ("what happened to me")
    - Hippocampus + prefrontal + temporal
    - "Mental time travel" — re-experience the past, simulate the future

  Semantic memory:
    - General world knowledge and facts ("Paris is in France")
    - Left temporal lobe, neocortex
    - Survives hippocampal damage better than episodic

IMPLICIT (NON-DECLARATIVE) MEMORY — unconscious:
  Procedural memory:
    - Motor skills, habits ("how to ride a bike")
    - Basal ganglia + cerebellum + motor cortex
    - Survives hippocampal damage (HM could still learn motor tasks)

  Priming:
    - Prior exposure facilitates future processing
    - Widespread cortical (perceptual, conceptual)

  Classical conditioning:
    - Stimulus-response associations
    - Amygdala (fear conditioning), cerebellum (eyeblink conditioning)

  Habituation/Sensitization:
    - Simplest learning forms
    - Localized to relevant sensory areas

WORKING MEMORY:
  - Active, online maintenance of information for immediate use
  - Capacity: ~7±2 "chunks" (Miller, 1956); modern estimate ~4 items
  - Neural basis: sustained firing in dlPFC, parietal, thalamus
  - Theta-gamma coupling coordinates WM content

MEMORY CONSOLIDATION:
  Systems consolidation:
    - Hippocampus gradually transfers memory traces to neocortex
    - Sleep (especially slow-wave sleep) is critical for consolidation
    - Takes days to years; older memories become hippocampus-independent

   Synaptic consolidation:
     - Protein synthesis required within ~6 hours of learning
     - Anisomycin blocks protein synthesis → blocks LTM formation
     - mRNA transport to synapses, CREB transcription factor activation

   CONSOLIDATION TIMELINE:
     Seconds–Minutes:   Early LTP; post-translational modifications
     Minutes–Hours:     Late LTP; protein synthesis; synaptic consolidation
     Days–Weeks:        Systems consolidation begins; hippocampal replay during sleep
     Months–Years:      Hippocampus-independent neocortical memory (fully consolidated)

   SYNAPTIC TAGGING & CAPTURE (Frey & Morris, 1997):
     - A synapse that undergoes LTP sets a "synaptic tag" (local molecular mark)
     - This tag captures plasticity-related proteins (PRPs) synthesized elsewhere
     - Enables weak inputs arriving near tagged synapses to also be strengthened
     - Explains how local, input-specific LTP can persist for hours
     - Key mechanism: protein synthesis in soma + capture at tagged synapses

FORGETTING:
  - Not just failure — active, adaptive process
  - Interference (proactive, retroactive)
  - Trace decay (contested; more likely interference)
  - Motivated forgetting (suppression)
  - Schema-based reconstruction errors
  - Retrieval-induced forgetting (remembering one thing suppresses related)
  (See Section 23 for sleep's role in consolidation.)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
18. ATTENTION & CONSCIOUSNESS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ATTENTION:
  - Selective amplification of relevant neural signals
  - Suppression of irrelevant signals

  Types:
    Spatial attention  → Where in space to focus
    Feature attention  → Which feature (color, motion) to focus on
    Object attention   → Which object to attend
    Temporal attention → When to expect a stimulus

  Neural mechanisms:
    - Top-down (endogenous): frontal/parietal → sensory cortex
    - Bottom-up (exogenous): salience-driven; amygdala + superior colliculus
    - Frontoparietal network (FPN): attentional control
    - Alpha suppression: attended area loses alpha; others maintain alpha
    - Feature Integration Theory: attention binds features into objects
    - See Section 47 for emulation of consciousness in AI.

CONSCIOUSNESS:
  The "Hard Problem" (Chalmers): Why is there subjective experience at all?
  The "Easy Problems": How does the brain integrate info, report states, control
  behavior? (These are hard but in principle solvable.)

  Major theories:
    Global Workspace Theory (Baars / Dehaene):
      - Consciousness = information broadcast to a "global workspace"
      - Long-range frontoparietal connections "ignite" → conscious access
      - Predicts: late cortical processing (300ms+), global synchrony

    Integrated Information Theory (Tononi, IIT):
      - Consciousness = integrated information (Φ, "phi")
      - High Φ = high consciousness
      - Physical substrate doesn't matter — what matters is the causal
        structure of information integration
      - Controversial: feedforward networks (like current AI) have Φ≈0

    Predictive Processing (Friston):
      - Brain as a prediction machine; constantly generating models
      - Consciousness = failed predictions reaching awareness
      - Free Energy Principle: brain minimizes surprise (free energy)

    Recurrent Processing Theory (Lamme):
      - Consciousness requires recurrent (feedback) loops in cortex
      - Feedforward sweep is not enough; needs recurrence

  Neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs):
    - P300 ERP component (conscious recognition)
    - Gamma synchrony (feature binding, conscious percepts)
    - Late cortical spreading activation (>300ms after stimulus)
    - Thalamocortical loops (disruption → unconsciousness in anesthesia)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
19. EMOTION & MOTIVATION
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

EMOTION — basic structure:
  Basic emotions (Ekman): Fear, Anger, Disgust, Happiness, Sadness, Surprise
  These are proposed to be universal across cultures (though debated)

  Two-factor theory (Schachter-Singer):
    Emotion = physiological arousal + cognitive label

  Somatic marker hypothesis (Damasio):
    Body states (somatic markers) guide decision-making
    vmPFC damage → flat affect + catastrophic decisions (Phineas Gage archetype)

MOTIVATION:
  Intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation
  Maslow's hierarchy (rough neural correlates):
    - Physiological: hypothalamus, brainstem
    - Safety: amygdala, ACC
    - Social: nucleus accumbens, oxytocin, insula
    - Esteem: MPFC, striatum
    - Self-actualization: PFC, default mode network

  DOPAMINE AND MOTIVATION:
    - Dopamine ≠ pleasure (debunked)
    - Dopamine = WANTING (incentive salience; Berridge)
    - Reward Prediction Error (RPE):
        Dopamine spike when reward > expected (positive RPE)
        Dopamine dip when reward < expected (negative RPE)
        No change when reward = expected
    - This is the biological implementation of Temporal Difference (TD) learning

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
20. LANGUAGE & COMMUNICATION
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Language is the most uniquely human cognitive faculty.

LANGUAGE AREAS:
  Broca's area (BA 44/45, left IFG):
    - Speech production, articulation, syntax
    - Subvocalizes grammar; processes hierarchical sentence structure
    - Damage → Broca's aphasia: effortful, telegraphic speech, intact comprehension

  Wernicke's area (BA 22, left STG):
    - Speech comprehension; sound-to-meaning mapping
    - Damage → Wernicke's aphasia: fluent but meaningless speech ("word salad")

  Arcuate fasciculus:
    - Connects Broca's and Wernicke's
    - Damage → Conduction aphasia: poor repetition, spared production/comprehension

  Angular gyrus (BA 39):
    - Reading, writing, arithmetic; semantic integration
    - Damage → alexia, agraphia, Gerstmann syndrome

LANGUAGE NETWORKS BEYOND THE CLASSICAL MODEL:
  - Ventral stream: sound → meaning (anterior temporal lobe)
  - Dorsal stream: sound → articulation (posterior planum temporale → frontal)
  - Right hemisphere: prosody, discourse, metaphor, pragmatics
  - Basal ganglia: fluency, rhythm, automaticity of speech
  - Cerebellum: timing of speech production

UNIVERSAL GRAMMAR (Chomsky):
  - All human languages share deep structural rules
  - Language acquisition device (LAD) is innate
  - Children acquire grammar without explicit instruction
  - Evidence: poverty of stimulus argument; universal stages of acquisition

LANGUAGE AND THOUGHT (Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis):
  - Strong version: language determines thought (discredited)
  - Weak version: language influences certain thought patterns (supported)
  - Color perception, spatial cognition, number concepts vary by language

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
21. PERCEPTION
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

VISION:
  Retina → LGN (thalamus) → V1 → V2 → Ventral/Dorsal streams
  - 30% of cortex involved in visual processing
  - What we "see" is largely a reconstruction/prediction, not raw input
  - Change blindness, inattentional blindness: vast amounts filtered out

HEARING:
  Cochlea (frequency → place code) → Auditory nerve → Cochlear nuclei →
  Superior olive (binaural comparison) → Inferior colliculus →
  Medial geniculate nucleus → A1 (tonotopic map) → Auditory association

TOUCH & PROPRIOCEPTION:
  Skin receptors (Meissner, Merkel, Ruffini, Pacinian) → Dorsal horn →
  Medial lemniscus → Thalamus (VPL) → S1 (somatotopic homunculus)
  Proprioception: muscle spindles, Golgi tendon organs → cerebellum + cortex

SMELL (OLFACTION):
  Only sense that bypasses the thalamus:
  Olfactory epithelium → Olfactory bulb → Piriform cortex / Amygdala
  Direct limbic access explains powerful emotion-smell memories

TASTE (GUSTATION):
  5 basic tastes: sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami
  Tongue receptors → CN VII, IX, X → Nucleus tractus solitarius →
  Thalamus (VPM) → Primary gustatory cortex (anterior insula / operculum)

MULTISENSORY INTEGRATION:
  - Brain constantly merges sensory channels (McGurk effect, rubber hand illusion)
  - Superior temporal sulcus (STS): major multisensory convergence zone
  - Temporal binding window: ~100-200ms synchrony = perceived as one event

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
22. MOTOR CONTROL
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

HIERARCHY OF MOTOR CONTROL:

  Level 3 (Strategic): Prefrontal cortex, posterior parietal cortex
    - Goal selection, action planning, when/why to move

  Level 2 (Tactical): Motor cortex (M1, PM, SMA), basal ganglia, cerebellum
    - How to move; sequence selection; error correction

  Level 1 (Execution): Spinal cord, brainstem motor nuclei
    - Muscle activation patterns; reflexes; central pattern generators

CORTICOSPINAL TRACT:
  M1 (layer V Betz cells) → Internal capsule → Brainstem → Decussate at
  medulla pyramids → Lateral CST → Contralateral anterior horn → Motor neurons
  → Neuromuscular junction → Muscle contraction
  (See Section 09 for cerebellum's role in motor learning.)

CEREBELLUM'S ROLE:
  - Receives: motor copy (efference copy) + sensory feedback
  - Computes: motor error; updates internal forward models
  - Outputs to: motor cortex via thalamus (VL nucleus)
  - Lesion: ataxia (uncoordinated movement), dysmetria (misjudging distance)

BASAL GANGLIA'S ROLE:
  - Select which motor program to run (action selection)
  - Suppress competing programs
  - Reinforce successful movements via dopamine
  - Lesion: Parkinson's (hypokinesia), Huntington's (hyperkinesia)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
23. SLEEP & THE BRAIN
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

SLEEP ARCHITECTURE:
  NREM Stage 1 (N1):  Drowsiness; theta waves; hypnic jerks
  NREM Stage 2 (N2):  Sleep spindles (12-15Hz bursts); K-complexes; ~50% total
  NREM Stage 3 (N3):  Slow-wave sleep (SWS); delta waves; deep sleep; restorative
  REM sleep:          Rapid eye movements; vivid dreams; motor paralysis;
                      EEG resembles wake; memory consolidation; emotional processing

  Sleep cycle: ~90 minutes; 4-6 cycles/night
  Early night: more SWS; Later night: more REM

FUNCTIONS OF SLEEP:
  Memory consolidation:    SWS: fact/spatial memory; REM: procedural/emotional
  Glymphatic clearance:    CSF flushes metabolic waste (amyloid-β, tau)
  Synaptic homeostasis:    SHY hypothesis: sleep downscales excess synapses
  Energy restoration:      ATP replenishment; glycogen stores restored
  Emotional regulation:    REM deactivates amygdala reactivity
  Immune function:         Cytokine release; sleep deprivation = immunosuppression
  (See Section 17 for memory consolidation during sleep.)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
24. THE AUTONOMIC NERVOUS SYSTEM
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

SYMPATHETIC (fight-or-flight):
  - Increases heart rate, dilates pupils, inhibits digestion
  - Releases adrenaline (epinephrine) from adrenal medulla
  - Hypothalamus → lateral horn of spinal cord → ganglia → organs
  - NT: norepinephrine (ganglionic: ACh, post-ganglionic: NE)

PARASYMPATHETIC (rest-and-digest):
  - Decreases heart rate, stimulates digestion, promotes recovery
  - Cranial nerves (especially vagus, CN X) + sacral spinal cord
  - NT: acetylcholine (both ganglionic and post-ganglionic)

VAGUS NERVE (CN X):
  - Carries 80% afferent (gut→brain) vs 20% efferent fibers
  - Major gut-brain axis pathway
  - Vagal tone: high = calm, parasympathetic dominance; low = stress
  - Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS): treats depression, epilepsy
  - Polyvagal theory (Porges): three states — safe, fight/flight, shutdown

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
25. COGNITIVE ABILITIES
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The human brain's cognitive repertoire is staggering:

  Intelligence:
    - General factor g (psychometric); fluid vs. crystallized
    - Multiple intelligences (Gardner): linguistic, logical-mathematical,
      spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal,
      naturalistic, existential
    - Neural efficiency hypothesis: smarter brains = less activation
    - Parieto-frontal integration theory (P-FIT): IQ linked to efficient
      communication between frontal and parietal areas

  Executive Functions (frontal lobe specialization):
    - Cognitive flexibility (task-switching, updating)
    - Inhibitory control (suppressing prepotent responses)
    - Working memory (online manipulation of information)
    - Planning (future-oriented goal decomposition)
    - Metacognition (thinking about thinking)

  Reasoning:
    - Deductive: valid conclusion from premises (syllogisms)
    - Inductive: generalizing from observations
    - Abductive: best explanation from evidence
    - Analogical: mapping structure between domains

  Fluid cognition:
    - Novel problem solving without learned patterns
    - Relies heavily on PFC, parietal, ACC

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
26. PATTERN RECOGNITION
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The brain is fundamentally a pattern recognition machine.

  Visual:     Object recognition in ~100-150ms (faster than thought)
  Auditory:   Speech recognition; musical structure detection
  Temporal:   Sequence detection; rhythm, causality
  Statistical: Probability tracking; Bayesian inference (implicit)
  Social:     Face reading; gaze detection; emotional state inference
  Linguistic: Syntax parsing; metaphor; pragmatics

PREDICTIVE PROCESSING:
  - Brain generates predictions at every level of the hierarchy
  - Only prediction ERRORS propagate upward (efficient coding)
  - Prior beliefs + incoming evidence → posterior (Bayesian update)
  - Hallucinations = perception driven by priors without sensory grounding
  - Delusions = failure to update beliefs despite disconfirming evidence

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
27. CREATIVITY & IMAGINATION
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Creativity = novel + valuable recombination of existing knowledge

  Neural basis:
    - Default Mode Network (DMN): spontaneous thought, divergent thinking
    - Executive Control Network (ECN): evaluation, refinement
    - Salience Network: switching between DMN and ECN
    - Creative individuals show higher DMN-ECN cooperation

  Divergent vs. Convergent thinking:
    Divergent: generating many possible solutions (DMN dominated)
    Convergent: finding the single correct solution (ECN dominated)
    Insight: gamma burst in right temporal lobe (~300ms before "aha!")

  Imagination:
    - Mental simulation using perceptual and motor systems
    - Imagining an action activates motor cortex
    - Imagining a visual scene activates V1 (weakly)
    - Episodic future thinking: hippocampus + PFC + DMN
    - Same machinery as memory retrieval (constructive nature of memory)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
28. SOCIAL COGNITION & THEORY OF MIND
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

SOCIAL BRAIN HYPOTHESIS:
  - Large human neocortex evolved primarily for social complexity
  - Dunbar's number (~150): cognitive limit for stable social groups
  - Mediated by PFC, temporal-parietal junction (TPJ), STS, amygdala

THEORY OF MIND (ToM) / MENTALIZING:
  - Ability to attribute mental states (beliefs, desires, intentions) to others
  - Key areas: mPFC, TPJ (bilateral), posterior STS, temporal poles
  - Absent/impaired in autism spectrum disorder
  - Level-1 ToM: "I know X" develops ~3-4 years
  - Level-2 ToM: "I know that you think X" develops ~5-6 years
  - Higher-order: "She knows that he knows that she knows..." → PFC load

MIRROR NEURONS:
  - Fire both when performing an action AND when observing it
  - Found in: premotor cortex (F5 in macaques; human homolog: BA44/45)
  - Role in imitation, empathy, language, social understanding
  - Broken mirror hypothesis of autism (disputed; oversimplified)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
29. DECISION MAKING & RISK ASSESSMENT
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

DUAL PROCESS THEORY (Kahneman):
  System 1: Fast, automatic, unconscious, heuristic, emotional
            (amygdala, basal ganglia, insula, ventral striatum)
  System 2: Slow, deliberate, conscious, rule-based, effortful
            (dlPFC, ACC, hippocampus)

VALUATION SYSTEMS:
  - vmPFC computes subjective value of options
  - Comparison of value signals drives choice
  - Time discounting: future rewards devalued (hyperbolic discounting)
  - Loss aversion: losses hurt ~2x more than equivalent gains feel good
    Neural: losses trigger amygdala + insula activation; gains → ventral striatum

RISK:
  - Insula signals risk; anterior insula active for uncertainty
  - Serotonin modulates risk aversion
  - Dopamine state affects risk preference (high DA → risk-seeking)
  - ACC monitors conflict and error; adjusts behavior post-error

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
30. LEARNING PARADIGMS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The brain implements multiple learning algorithms simultaneously:

  Associative learning:    Pavlovian (stimulus-stimulus) + Operant (response-outcome)
  Supervised learning:     Cerebellum (error signals from climbing fibers)
  Reinforcement learning:  Dopamine as reward prediction error signal (basal ganglia)
  Unsupervised learning:   Cortex extracts statistical regularities
  Hebbian learning:        LTP/LTD at synaptic level (co-activation)
  Observational learning:  Mirror system; watching others
  Insight learning:        Sudden reorganization of knowledge (Gestalt)
  Transfer learning:       Using knowledge from one domain in another
  Meta-learning:           Learning how to learn faster

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
31. ABSTRACT REASONING & MATHEMATICS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  Number sense (ANS): Approximate Number System exists from birth; bilateral
    intraparietal sulcus (IPS)
  Exact arithmetic: Left angular gyrus; language-dependent
  Higher mathematics: Prefrontal, parietal, temporal; requires abstraction
  Logic: Left prefrontal cortex; working memory + rule application
  Spatial reasoning: Right parietal cortex; visuospatial manipulation
  Formal systems: PFC for rules + hippocampus for composition

  Savant mathematics: Calendar calculation, prime factoring without apparent
    strategy — suggests parallel unconscious computation pathways

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
32. LIMITS OF THE HUMAN BRAIN
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Despite its power, the brain has hard limits:

  Working memory:     ~4 chunks simultaneous; ~20-30 seconds without rehearsal
  Attentional focus:  One demanding task at a time (multitasking = rapid switching)
  Processing speed:   ~120 ms minimum for conscious visual perception
  Pattern matching:   Great with familiar categories; terrible with edge cases
  Statistical reasoning: Terrible by default (Bayesian = hard; needs training)
  Cognitive biases:   100+ documented biases (availability, confirmation, etc.)
  Change blindness:   Enormous changes missed if attention not directed
  Ego depletion:      Decision quality declines after sustained cognitive effort
  Sleep deprivation:  24h awake = legal BAC 0.10% equivalent impairment
  Emotional override: Amygdala hijack — emotions can completely overwhelm reason
  Information overload: ~120 bits/sec maximum conscious processing bandwidth
  Forgetting curve:   ~70% forgotten within 24h without reinforcement (Ebbinghaus)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
33. THE BRAIN UNDER STRESS, FEAR, AND TRAUMA
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ACUTE STRESS RESPONSE:
  Threat detected (amygdala) → Hypothalamus → SAM axis (fast):
    Sympathetic nervous system + adrenal medulla → adrenaline + noradrenaline
    → Heart rate ↑, respiration ↑, muscles primed, digestion halted

  Hypothalamus → HPA axis (slow):
    CRH → pituitary → ACTH → adrenal cortex → cortisol (glucocorticoid)
    → Mobilizes glucose, suppresses immune/reproductive systems
    → Modulates hippocampus and amygdala

CHRONIC STRESS:
  - Prolonged cortisol → hippocampal atrophy (memory impairment)
  - Amygdala hyperactivation (anxiety, fear generalization)
  - Prefrontal weakening (reduced inhibition, poor decision-making)
  - Neurogenesis suppression

TRAUMA & PTSD:
  - Fear memory encoded so strongly it resists extinction
  - Hyperactive amygdala; underactive vmPFC (cannot extinguish fear)
  - Hippocampal fragmentation of trauma memory (flashbacks, intrusions)
  - Increased norepinephrine sensitivity
  - Treatment: exposure therapy, EMDR, pharmacology (propranolol), MDMA-assisted

34. ALTERED STATES OF CONSCIOUSNESS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  Meditation:      Focused attention → reduced DMN; gamma synchrony; long-term

  Flow state:      Transient hypofrontality; reduced self-monitoring; optimal

35. BRAIN DISORDERS & WHAT THEY REVEAL
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Disorders are natural experiments — lesions reveal function by subtraction.

  Alzheimer's disease:   Amyloid plaques, tau tangles; hippocampus first
                         → episodic memory loss first; then semantic, then skills

  Parkinson's disease:   Dopaminergic substantia nigra degeneration
                         → tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia; later cognitive decline

  Schizophrenia:         Excess dopamine (mesolimbic); PFC hypofunction
                         → positive symptoms (hallucinations) + negative (flat affect)

  Depression:            Serotonin/NE/dopamine imbalance; hippocampal atrophy;
                         hyperactive amygdala; subgenual ACC overactivation

  Autism Spectrum:       Connectivity differences; social cognition network;
                         sensory processing differences; local overconnectivity

  OCD:                   Cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loop hyperactivity
                         → intrusive thoughts + compulsive behaviors

  ADHD:                  Dopamine/NE dysregulation in PFC + striatum
                         → attention, impulsivity, executive function deficits

  Split-brain:           Corpus callosum severed; left/right hemispheres act
                         independently; reveals modular hemispheric specializations

  Capgras syndrome:      Face recognition OK; emotional response to faces cut off
                         → familiar people feel like impostors (amygdala disconnection)

  Cotard delusion:       Belief one is dead / doesn't exist; depersonalization extreme

  Foreign Accent Syndrome: Accent change after motor cortex/basal ganglia damage
  Synesthesia:           Cross-activation between sensory cortices

36. THE PHILOSOPHY OF MIND — CAN A BRAIN BE COPIED?
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This is where we enter contested territory. Before building, you must understand
the philosophical landscape.

WHAT WOULD "DIGITIZING A BRAIN" MEAN?

  Option A: Functional Emulation
    Replicate the input-output BEHAVIOR of the brain in a computer.
    Does NOT require understanding consciousness. Just get the function right.
    This is the engineering approach. Most achievable.

  Option B: Substrate-Independent Mind
    The mind IS the pattern of information processing. If you copy the pattern
    to a different substrate (silicon), the mind continues to exist.
    This is the functionalist position.

  Option C: Substrate-Dependent Consciousness
    Consciousness requires biological tissue (or specific physical processes).
    Copying to silicon produces a philosophical zombie — behaviorally identical
    but with no inner experience. Your "copy" wouldn't be you.

  Option D: Uploading as Death
    Any "copy" is a copy, not a continuation. The original brain would have to
    be destroyed to avoid two versions. Teleportation paradox applies.
    (See Section 47 for consciousness emulation challenges.)

KEY POSITIONS:
  Functionalism:     Mind is substrate-independent information processing
  Biological naturalism (Searle): Consciousness requires specific biology
  Panpsychism:       Consciousness is a fundamental property of matter
  Illusionism:       Consciousness as we conceive it is an illusion
  Dualism:           Mind and matter are ontologically separate (unfalsifiable)
  Physicalism:       Everything about mind is ultimately physical

For the engineering goal of brain digitalization, we adopt FUNCTIONALISM:
if we replicate the computational structure, we replicate the mind.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
37. WHOLE BRAIN EMULATION (WBE) — THE ROADMAP
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Whole Brain Emulation (WBE) is the most ambitious form of brain digitalization.
The Sandberg-Bostrom (2008) FHI technical report "Whole Brain Emulation: A
Roadmap" is the canonical reference. Here is the distilled pipeline:

STEP 1: Preservation (or In-Vivo Imaging)
  Option A (Destructive):
    Chemical fixation (glutaraldehyde, osmium, etc.) + embedding in resin
    Physical state preserved; biological activity stopped; then slice + image

  Option B (Non-Destructive — not yet feasible at full resolution):
    Ultra-high-field MRI (7T, 14T) for macrostructure
    X-ray phase contrast or synchrotron for finer structure
    Currently achievable at ~1-5 micron resolution (need ~10-20 nanometer)

STEP 2: Sectioning / Physical Sampling
  - Brain sliced into ultra-thin sections (30–100 nm for electron microscopy)
  - Automated Tape-Collecting Ultramicrotome (ATUM) — serial sections
  - Scale: ~10 million sections for a full human brain (1mm brain / 100nm)

STEP 3: High-Resolution Imaging
  - 3D Electron Microscopy (EM): most practical for nanoscale synaptic resolution
  - Serial Section Transmission EM (ssTEM)
  - Focused Ion Beam Scanning EM (FIB-SEM)
  - Current scale: ~1mm³ volumes (Harvard/Google Janelia: full fly brain in 2020;
    ~1mm³ human cortex mapped 2024)
  - Challenge: petabytes to exabytes of raw image data per brain

STEP 4: Image Processing & Segmentation
  - Automated segmentation of neurons, axons, dendrites, synapses
  - Deep learning (U-Net, FFN: Flood-Filling Networks by Google)
  - Identify cell types, organelles, synaptic vesicle counts, synaptic weights
  - Error rate: must be <1 per mm of axon to avoid cascading topological errors

STEP 5: Connectome Extraction
  - Build a graph: nodes = neurons, edges = synapses
  - Record synapse type (glutamate/GABA), location, estimated strength
  - Capture gap junctions (electrical synapses)
  - Includes glial cell connectivity? Highly debated whether necessary
  (See Section 50 for the full digitalization pipeline.)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
38. CONNECTOMICS — MAPPING EVERY CONNECTION
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Connectomics is the science of producing maps of neural connections in the
nervous system.

SCALES:
  Macroscale:   DTI tractography (MRI-based); white matter tracts; regions
  Mesoscale:    Viral tracing, light-sheet microscopy; inter-area axons
  Microscale:   EM-based; individual synapses; full wiring diagram

MILESTONES:
  1986:  C. elegans (nematode) full connectome — 302 neurons, 7,000 synapses
         (Sydney Brenner, John White) — took 12 years
  2020:  Full Drosophila (fruit fly) brain — 25,000 neurons, 20M synapses
         (FlyEM project, Janelia/Google)
  2021:  1mm³ human cortex — 57,000 cells, 150 million synapses, 1.4 petabytes
         (Harvard + Google Brain)
  2024+: Active work toward full mouse brain (~71 million neurons)
  ~2030-2050 (projection): Full human brain connectome

DATA CHALLENGE:
  1mm³ human cortex = 1.4 petabytes
  Full human brain (~1,300,000 mm³) = ~1.8 exabytes at the same density
  This is an informational challenge on par with global internet archives.

39. NEURAL DATA ACQUISITION METHODS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

For non-destructive (in-vivo) brain reading — necessary for "uploading" a
living person's brain — current methods and their limits:

  EEG (Electroencephalography):
    - Scalp electrodes; measures aggregate cortical oscillations
    - Temporal resolution: ~1ms (excellent)
    - Spatial resolution: ~1-2 cm (very poor)
    - Non-invasive, cheap, portable
    - Cannot resolve individual neurons

  fMRI (Functional MRI):
    - Measures BOLD signal (blood-oxygen-level-dependent)
    - Temporal resolution: ~2-3 seconds (poor — hemodynamics lag)
    - Spatial resolution: ~1-3mm voxels (moderate)
    - Expensive, stationary, noisy; indirectly measures neural activity
    - Cannot read out thought at neuronal level

  MEG (Magnetoencephalography):
    - Measures magnetic fields from neural currents
    - Temporal: ~1ms (good); Spatial: ~5mm (moderate)
    - Expensive; requires shielded room; non-invasive

  ECoG (Electrocorticography):
    - Electrode grid on cortical surface (requires craniotomy)
    - Temporal: ~1ms; Spatial: ~1mm
    - High signal quality; used in BCI (brain-computer interface) research

  Utah Array / Neuropixels:
    - Penetrating electrode arrays into cortex
    - Record individual spikes from hundreds to thousands of neurons
    - Neuropixels: 960 electrodes on 1 shaft; 30,000 neurons/cm²
    - Invasive; tissue damage; long-term stability issues

  Two-Photon Calcium Imaging:
    - Fluorescent indicators in neurons; flash when neuron fires (Ca²⁺ influx)
    - Can image tens of thousands of neurons simultaneously in mouse
    - Requires transparent window; not feasible in human cortex at depth

  Expansion Microscopy:
    - Chemically expand tissue ~4x → image with standard light microscope
    - Can achieve ~70nm resolution without EM
    - Destructive; combined with connectomics

  X-ray Holography / Synchrotron:
    - Non-destructive imaging at ~10nm resolution possible in small volumes
    - Beam time is limited; not scalable to full brain currently

  THE GAP:
    The fundamental gap between current non-invasive methods (~mm resolution)
    and required resolution (~10nm for synapses) is a factor of 100,000.
    This gap is the central technical barrier to non-destructive brain uploading.
  (See Section 41 for computational requirements of each fidelity level.)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
40. BIOLOGICAL FIDELITY LEVELS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A key design decision is: how faithful does the emulation need to be?

FIDELITY LEVELS (from Sandberg-Bostrom taxonomy):

  Level 1: Whole Brain Dynamics (coarse)
    - Capture inter-area connection weights and dynamics
    - Simulate with rate-coded neural mass models
    - Achievable with today's structural MRI + diffusion tractography
    - Fidelity: behavior will diverge rapidly; no individual memories

  Level 2: Mesoscale Column Simulation
    - Capture connectivity between cortical columns (~50,000 neurons each)
    - Rate-coded or simplified spiking models per column
    - Current connectome data + significant modeling assumptions
    - Fidelity: capture some personality/cognitive traits (weakly)

  Level 3: Individual Neuron + Synapse Simulation
    - Full list of every neuron and synapse with approximate weights
    - Simulate with simplified spiking neuron models (LIF, Izhikevich)
    - Requires micro-connectome (EM-based); ~2030s-2040s projection
    - Fidelity: preserves much cognitive structure; may miss sub-cellular detail

  Level 4: Full Biophysical Simulation (ion channel level)
    - Each neuron modeled with full Hodgkin-Huxley ion channel dynamics
    - Dendritic computation, receptor distributions, glial interactions
    - Likely needed for true fidelity (active dendrites are computational)
    - Requires ~100-1000x more compute than Level 3
    - Fidelity: near-perfect structural replication

  Level 5: Molecular Simulation
    - Protein folding, receptor dynamics, second-messenger cascades
    - MD simulation of synaptic transmission
    - Computationally intractable for full brain currently
    - ~10^25 operations for 1 second of full molecular brain simulation

PRACTICAL TARGET: Level 3-4 is the goal for meaningful brain digitalization.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
41. COMPUTATIONAL REQUIREMENTS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

NEURONS:     ~86 billion
SYNAPSES:    ~150 trillion
SPIKE RATE:  ~10 Hz average (but up to 100 Hz; ~0.1 Hz in many neocortical cells)

OPERATIONS TO SIMULATE:

  Simplified (LIF neurons, no dendritic computation):
    - ~10^14 synaptic operations per second (100 trillion ops/s)
    - Modern supercomputers: ~10^18 FLOPS (exaFLOP)
    - Seems feasible? But: each synaptic event ≠ 1 FLOP in simulation
    - Realistic cost: ~10^18 – 10^21 ops/sec for real-time simulation
    - Frontier (2024): ~1.2 exaFLOPs — borderline for highly simplified model

  Realistic (HH ion channel level):
    - ~10^4 differential equations per neuron × 86 billion neurons
    - ~10^15 differential equations total
    - At 1000 timesteps/second → ~10^18 equations solved per second
    - Wall time vs. real time ratio: ~10^6 – 10^9 (millions of times slower)
    - Projection: real-time at HH level requires ~10^25 FLOPS
    - Current best: ~10^18 FLOPS → still 10 million times underpowered

MEMORY REQUIREMENTS:
    - Connectivity matrix: 150 trillion synapses × 4 bytes = 600 terabytes min
    - Synaptic state (dynamic): × 10-100 = 6-60 petabytes working memory
    - Compressed neural codes may reduce by ~100x

ENERGY:
    - Blue Brain Project simulating 1mm³ (31,000 neurons): ~1 megawatt
    - Human brain: 20 watts
    - Current simulation is ~50,000x less energy efficient than biology

PROJECTION TIMELINE:
    - Law of accelerating returns (Kurzweil) suggests ~2030s-2040s
    - More conservative estimates: 2050-2100
    - Neuromorphic hardware may dramatically change these numbers

  4. Grounded sensorimotor embodiment
  5. Continuous, always-on background processing (no explicit inference calls)
  6. Intrinsic motivation, homeostatic drives
  7. Developmental learning from scratch (not pretraining on internet text)
  8. Physical world causality model

43. CURRENT AI ARCHITECTURES INSPIRED BY THE BRAIN
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TRANSFORMERS & ATTENTION:
  - Multi-head attention: loosely inspired by biological attention
  - Allows dynamic routing of information (cf. thalamic gating)
  - KV cache: rough analog to short-term memory buffer
  - Lack: biological attention is sparse; Transformer attention is dense/expensive

CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORKS (CNNs):
  - Directly inspired by Hubel & Wiesel's simple/complex cells in V1
  - Receptive fields, shared weights, hierarchical feature detection
  - Deep CNN hierarchy maps onto V1→V2→V4→IT visual hierarchy

RECURRENT NEURAL NETWORKS (RNNs / LSTMs / GRUs):
  - Temporal processing; sequence learning
  - Hidden state ≈ short-term memory (compressed context)
  - LSTM gates (forget, input, output) ≈ modulatory control of memory

HOPFIELD NETWORKS / ASSOCIATIVE MEMORY:
  - Attractor dynamics (multiple stable states) ≈ memory retrieval
  - Modern Hopfield networks (Ramsauer 2020): used in Transformer layers
  - CAM3 (context-addressable memory) ≈ hippocampal pattern completion

GENERATIVE ADVERSARIAL NETWORKS (GANs):
  - Generator-discriminator: possible analogy to predictive coding
  - Generator = brain's top-down prediction; discriminator = error signal

VARIATIONAL AUTOENCODERS (VAEs):
  - Learn latent representations; generate by sampling from latent space
  - Latent space ≈ compressed world model representation in cortex

WORLD MODELS (Ha & Schmidhuber, Hafner):
  - Explicit forward model of environment dynamics
  - Dream in latent space; plan before acting
  - DreamerV3: learn all with single algorithm; ≈ cerebellar forward model

PREDICTIVE CODING NETWORKS (Rao & Ballard):
  - Hierarchical predictions; only errors propagate up
  - Exact mathematical implementation of Friston's predictive processing
  - Current limitation: hard to scale like backprop-trained networks

FREE ENERGY MINIMIZATION (Friston / Active Inference):
   - Unified framework: perception + action as minimizing prediction error
   - Agents take actions to confirm their predictions (not just update)
   - Being implemented in robotics and AI agents (pymdp library)

COGNITIVE ARCHITECTURES (ACT-R, SOAR):
   (See Section 44 for spiking neural network implementations.)
   ACT-R (Anderson, 1993):
     - Hybrid architecture: symbolic modules + subsymbolic activation dynamics
     - Modules: visual, manual, declarative, procedural, goal, imaginal
     - Each module operates in parallel; central production system serializes
     - Declarative memory chunks: activation-based retrieval (base-level + spreading)
     - Procedural memory: production rules with utility learning
     - Sub-symbolic parameters: activation decay, noise, threshold
     - Biologically inspired by prefrontal + basal ganglia + hippocampal loops
     - Validated against human reaction times, fMRI activation patterns, error rates
     - Extended to ACT-R/Φ for cognitive neuroscience predictions

   SOAR (Laird, 1987):
     - Problem-space based architecture; universal subgoaling
     - Chunking as learning mechanism (compiles experience into rules)
     - Used in AI agents, game AI, cognitive modeling

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
44. SPIKING NEURAL NETWORKS (SNNs)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

SNNs are the third generation of neural networks — closest to biological reality.

KEY FEATURES:
  - Neurons communicate via discrete spikes (binary events in time)
  - Spike timing matters (not just rate)
  - Sparse activation (only a fraction of neurons fire at any moment)
  - Natural integration of temporal dynamics
  - Massive energy efficiency on neuromorphic hardware

NEURON MODELS (in order of biological accuracy vs. efficiency):
  Integrate-and-Fire (IF):     Simplest; integrate input until threshold → spike
  Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF): Adds membrane leak; most widely used
  Adaptive LIF (ALIF):          Adds spike-frequency adaptation
  Izhikevich (2003):            2 equations; reproduces all known neuron types
  Hodgkin-Huxley (HH, 1952):   Full ion channel biophysics; most accurate; expensive
  Multi-Compartment models:     Dendrites explicitly modeled; spatial computation

LEARNING IN SNNs:
  Spike-timing dependent plasticity (STDP):
    - If pre fires BEFORE post: strengthen (LTP) (within ~20ms window)
    - If pre fires AFTER post: weaken (LTD)
    - Unsupervised; biologically plausible; but limited task performance

  Backpropagation-through-time (BPTT) with surrogate gradients:
    - Approximate gradient of non-differentiable spike function
    - Enables powerful supervised training (used in competitive SNNs today)

  Online local learning (e-prop, OSTL):
    - Biologically plausible; doesn't require storing full time history
    - Forward-mode differentiation; eligibility traces

CURRENT SNN BENCHMARKS (2024):
  - Competitive with ANNs on audio tasks (N-MINIST, SHD dataset)
  - Still behind Transformers on complex language tasks
  - 10-100x more energy efficient on neuromorphic hardware for suitable tasks
  - Active research: SpyTorch, snnTorch, BindsNET, Norse (Python frameworks)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
45. NEUROMORPHIC HARDWARE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Neuromorphic chips implement brain-inspired computation in silicon.
The goal: achieve the brain's ~20 watts with brain-scale computation.

  Intel Loihi 2 (2021):
    - 1 million synapses per chip; 128 neuromorphic cores
    - On-chip STDP learning; real-time event-driven processing
    - Ultra-low power for pattern recognition, robotics, sensory processing

  IBM TrueNorth (2014):
    - 1 million neurons; 256 million synapses; 4096 cores
    - 65mW power; designed for inference only (no on-chip learning)

  SpiNNaker 2 (Manchester):
    - Massively parallel; simulate large-scale SNN models
    - Goal: mouse-brain scale simulation in real time
    - Used for research; not production AI deployment

  BrainScaleS (Heidelberg):
    - Analog neuromorphic; runs 10,000x faster than real time
    - Physical emulation using transistors as analog neurons
    - Useful for fast simulation of brain dynamics

  Mythic AI / Analog ML:
    - Analog in-memory computation; weights stored as conductances
    - No data movement between memory and compute (solves Von Neumann bottleneck)

  FUTURE: Memristive crossbars, phase-change memory, photonic neuromorphics
    could enable molecular-scale synaptic integration

THE VON NEUMANN BOTTLENECK vs. THE BRAIN:
  Standard CPUs/GPUs: compute and memory are separate → massive energy wasted
  moving data between them. The brain has NO separation — computation happens
  AT the synapse. Memristors attempt to replicate this: resistance = weight;
  current × voltage = multiply-accumulate. One atomic-scale synapse per device.

46. MEMORY SYSTEMS IN AI VS. IN BIOLOGY
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  ┌───────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ BIOLOGICAL            │ AI EQUIVALENT                                │
  ├───────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Working memory (WM)   │ Context window / KV cache / hidden state     │
  │ Episodic memory       │ RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)         │
  │                       │ + vector database (FAISS, ChromaDB, etc.)    │
  │ Semantic memory       │ Model weights (in-context encoded knowledge) │
  │ Procedural memory     │ Trained model weights for skill execution    │
  │ Priming               │ Activation patterns; in-context examples     │
  │ Consolidation (sleep) │ Fine-tuning epochs; offline training         │
  │ Forgetting            │ Weight decay; context window cutoff          │
  │ Pattern completion    │ Associative retrieval; attention             │
  │ Pattern separation    │ Orthogonal representations; decorrelation    │
  └───────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────┘

COMPLEMENTARY LEARNING SYSTEMS (CLS) THEORY:
  - Hippocampus: fast, specific, one-shot encoding (new episodes)
  - Neocortex: slow, statistical, abstract generalization
  - Interplay avoids catastrophic forgetting
  - AI analog: memory-augmented networks (NTM, DNC, MemoryTransformer)
  - Replay during sleep: stochastic reactivation of hippocampal traces
    → cortical integration (generalization)
  - AI analog: Experience Replay in RL; generative replay (GAN-based)

CONTINUAL LEARNING IN AI (active challenge):
  - Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC): protect important weights
  - Progressive Neural Networks: grow new columns per task
  - PackNet: binary masking of weights per task
  - MERLIN / HBP: memory-based architectures inspired by CLS

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
47. EMULATING CONSCIOUSNESS — THE HARD PROBLEM
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This section is necessarily speculative — we do not have a scientific theory
of consciousness sufficient to engineer it. However:

WHAT WE KNOW WE CAN REPLICATE:
  - Information processing (behavior, cognition, perception)
  - Memory systems
  - Attention, arousal, sleep-like dynamics
  - Emotional valence tagging (reward/punishment signals)
  - Language and reasoning
  - Goal-directed behavior

WHAT WE CANNOT (YET) VERIFY OR ENGINEER:
  - Qualia (subjective "redness" of red; "painfulness" of pain)
  - Self-model with genuine first-person perspective
  - Phenomenal unity (binding of diverse experience into one "now")

APPROACHES TO ENGINEERING CONSCIOUS-LIKE SYSTEMS:

  Global Workspace Architecture:
    - Implement a "blackboard" architecture where multiple specialist modules
      compete to broadcast to a global workspace
    - Baars/Dehaene → leads to unified, attention-limited information access
    - Franklin's LIDA (Learning Intelligent Distribution Agent) does this
    - Does NOT guarantee qualia — only functional consciousness

  Integrated Information Architecture (Φ):
    - Maximize Φ in network architecture
    - Avoid purely feedforward structure (Φ≈0)
    - Use recurrent, tightly integrated networks
    - Problems: Φ is computationally intractable to calculate at scale

  Attention Schema Theory (Graziano):
    - Consciousness = brain model of its own attention process
    - Build an AI that has an accurate model of its own computational state
    - "Attention Schema" in AI = meta-model of what the system is focusing on

  Predictive Self-Modeling:
    - System builds a predictive model of itself in the world
    - Errors between predicted and actual self-state = proto-selfhood
    - Leads to drives, curiosity, nociception (pain-analog)

HONEST ASSESSMENT:
  Even a perfect neuron-by-neuron simulation running in silicon might:
  A) Be conscious with identical experience (functionalism)
  B) Be a philosophical zombie (biological naturalism)
  We currently have no way to measure this from outside.
  The Turing Test tests behavior, not qualia.

    Gene expression maps + connectivity atlas across mouse and human brain
    Cell type classification; brain region transcriptomics

  BigBrain Project:
    Ultra-high resolution 3D human brain atlas at 20-micrometer resolution
    (digitized histological slices; not EM-level)

  FlyEM / Princeton MBL / Google Brain:
    Full Drosophila connectome (2020); mouse retina; partial mouse cortex
    Using FAIM + deep learning automated segmentation

BRAIN SIMULATION:
  Blue Brain Project (EPFL, Henry Markram):
    - Simulate reconstructed neocortical columns of rat brain
    - Full biophysical simulation (Hodgkin-Huxley; ~31,000 neurons / 1mm³)
    - Blue Brain 5 supercomputer; ~5 petaFLOP
    - Published first detailed digital rat neocortical column (2015)
     - Controversy: criticized for lack of emergent behavior claims
     - See Section 40 for fidelity classification of this approach.

  Human Brain Project (HBP, EU €1B):
    - Multi-lab EU megaproject; multiple simulators, hardware, data platforms
    - EBRAINS platform: open tools for brain research
    - Mixed results; management controversies; now transitioning to EBRAINS2.0

  OpenWorm:
    - Full simulation of C. elegans (~302 neurons) in software
    - Connectome uploaded to Lego robot → autonomous behavior
    (See Section 37 for the overall WBE roadmap.)

49. ETHICAL & PHILOSOPHICAL IMPLICATIONS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

If brain digitalization becomes possible, the ethical landscape is staggering.

IDENTITY & CONTINUITY:
  - If you are copied to silicon, is the copy "you"?
  - If original brain is destroyed: murder or suicide?
  - If both survive: two instances of you exist simultaneously — which one is "you"?
  - Gradual neuron replacement (like Theseus's ship): at what point does identity shift?
  - Legal status: is a brain upload a person? A product? Property?

COPYING & PIRACY:
  - Could an uploaded mind be duplicated, run multiple times, sold?
  - Could it be modified against its will (editing memories, values)?
  - Corporate ownership of uploaded minds: dystopia scenario

SUBJECTIVE EXPERIENCE IN SIMULATION:
  - Is a simulated mind suffering if its simulation contains suffering states?
  - Moral status of digital minds: utilitarian calculations change entirely
  - Billions of digital copies could vastly outnumber biological humans

INEQUALITY:
  - Upload technology will be extraordinarily expensive initially
  - Will create a new class divide: mortal vs. potentially immortal
  - Military implications: killable vs. unkillable soldiers (backups)

CONSCIOUSNESS EXTINCTION:
  - Could uploading actually destroy consciousness (not transfer it)?
  - If biological naturalism is correct, every "upload" is a murder +
    creation of a behavioral clone

REGULATORY GAPS:
  - No existing legal framework covers mind uploading, digital personhood,
    neural data rights, or connectome privacy

PRIVACY:
  - A full connectome is the ultimate personal data: thoughts, memories,
    personality, desires — everything
  - Who controls access? Who gets to decrypt a dead person's connectome?

50. A PRACTICAL BLUEPRINT — STEP-BY-STEP BRAIN DIGITALIZATION PIPELINE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This is a synthesis of everything above into a concrete engineering blueprint
for what brain digitalization would look like when the technology is ready.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
PHASE 1: CAPTURE
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Step 1.1 — Preservation
  Method A (Destructive, maximum fidelity):
    - Rapid intravascular perfusion fixation with glutaraldehyde + OsO4
    - Cryoprotection with DMSO solution or glycerol
    - Vitrification in liquid nitrogen (no ice crystal damage)
    - Or: Aldehyde-stabilized cryopreservation (Nectome protocol)

  Method B (Non-destructive, future tech):
    - Ultra-fast molecular freeze (ns timescale) to halt all activity
    - Full-state snapshot of electrochemical state + molecular state
    - Currently science fiction

Step 1.2 — Physical Sectioning
  - ATUM (Automated Tape-Collecting Ultramicrotome): 30nm sections
  - Label sections with heavy metal stains (OsO4 for myelin, lead for organelles)
  - Organize ~10 million sections (for full human brain) on tape substrate

Step 1.3 — Imaging
  - Multi-beam scanning EM array (e.g., ZEISS MultiSEM: 61 beams parallel)
  - Resolution: 4nm × 4nm × 30nm per voxel
  - Speed: ~10 ms/µm² → full brain: ~100 years single beam; ~2 years multi-beam
  - Projected hardware: 1000-beam array, 2 hour/full-brain by 2040s (estimated)
  - Data output: ~2 exabytes raw image data

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
PHASE 2: RECONSTRUCTION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Step 2.1 — Image Alignment
  - Register sections to correct for mechanical drift/section artifacts
  - Deep learning-based non-rigid registration (e.g., SOFIMA by Google)

Step 2.2 — Segmentation
  - 3D deep learning segmentation (U-Net / FFN) to identify:
      • Every neuron's cell body, axon, dendritic tree
      • Every synapse (pre- and post-synaptic density)
      • Glial cells (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes)
      • Myelin sheaths and gaps
  - Estimated parameters to extract per synapse:
      • Location (x,y,z) • Vesicle count • Active zone area
      • Synaptic cleft width • Receptor density estimates
      • NT type (from ultrastructure + molecular labeling)

Step 2.3 — Connectome Graph Assembly
  - Build graph: nodes (neurons with properties) + edges (synapses with weights)
  - Node properties: cell type, location, morphology, receptor expression
  - Edge properties: synapse type, weight, AMPA/NMDA ratio, release probability
  - Size: ~86 billion nodes, ~150 trillion edges
  - Storage: compressed ~10-50 petabytes (edge list with properties)

Step 2.4 — Molecular Augmentation (if data available)
  - Correlative CLEM (Correlative Light/EM): overlay fluorescence + EM data
  - smFISH / MERFISH: spatial transcriptomics per cell → gene expression profile
    → infer receptor types, neuron subtypes, modulatory inputs
  - Proteomics: mass spec of synaptic proteome

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
PHASE 3: SIMULATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Step 3.1 — Assign Neuron Models
  - Classify each neuron into one of ~hundreds of known cell types
    (based on morphology + gene expression)
  - Assign appropriate computational model per cell type:
      Excitatory pyramidal → AdEx or Izhikevich with layer-specific parameters
      PV interneurons → Fast-spiking LIF (short refractory, narrow spikes)
      Purkinje cells → Multi-compartment model (obligatory)
      Dopamine neurons → DA neuron model (pacemaker, burst-pause dynamics)
  - Source: Allen Cell Types Database, Blue Brain cell model library

Step 3.2 — Parameterize Synaptic Models
  - Tsodyks-Markram model for short-term plasticity (depression/facilitation)
  - STDP rules initialized from species-average; refined by activity
  - Modulatory system implementation:
      DA, 5-HT, NE, ACh: implement as diffuse volume transmission
      Each modulates ion channel kinetics in target regions

Step 3.3 — Initialize State
  - The hardest problem: what is the initial state of every ion channel?
  - Option A: Initialize at resting potential → simulate for "warm-up" period
    → behavior will diverge from original brain
  - Option B: Capture electrophysiological state at moment of preservation
    (requires in-vivo high-resolution measurement → currently impossible)
  - Option C: Accept that re-initialization creates a "fresh" instance of the
    structural brain, not a moment-in-time copy

Step 3.4 — Simulation Platform
  Hardware:
    - Neuromorphic (preferred for energy): Intel Loihi 2 cluster / future BrainScaleS
    - GPU cluster (current pragmatic option): A100/H100 array
    - Target: real-time simulation at LIF level; 1000x-slow at HH level

  Software:
    - NEURON simulator (Yale): gold standard for single-neuron biophysics
    - NEST simulator: network-scale SNN; millions of neurons
    - Brian2: Python-based; flexible; good for research
    - CoreNEURON: HPC-optimized NEURON backend
    - Custom SNN (e.g., GeNN: GPU-enhanced Neuronal Networks)

Step 3.5 — Glial Integration (optional but important)
  - Astrocyte calcium dynamics: modulate synaptic transmission
  - Tripartite synapse model: pre + post + astrocyte
  - Simplified: model astrocytes as slow modulatory fields
  - Advanced: full calcium wave dynamics (computationally expensive)

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
PHASE 4: EMBODIMENT & INTERFACE
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Step 4.1 — Sensory Input
  - Map sensory organs to appropriate cortical input areas
  - Option A: Virtual environment (simulated sensors)
  - Option B: Real-world sensors (camera → V1; microphone → A1; etc.)
  - Transform raw input into neural spike patterns at sensory cortex

Step 4.2 — Motor Output
  - Read out spike patterns from motor cortex layer V
  - Decode intended movement via population vector decoding
  - Send to: physical robot actuators, virtual avatar, text output

Step 4.3 — Internal Homeostasis
  - Implement hypothalamic drives: simulate hunger, fatigue, circadian
  - Modulator levels: implement dopamine, serotonin, NE, ACh tone
    as global parameters that shift dynamically

Step 4.4 — I/O Bandwidth
  - Goal: same effective information exchange as biological brain
  - ~120 bits/sec conscious bandwidth (easy)
  - Full sensorimotor: high-dimensional; scale with available hardware

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
PHASE 5: VALIDATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Step 5.1 — Behavioral Validation
  - Does the emulation exhibit behavior consistent with the original person?
  - Personality tests; cognitive benchmarks; autobiographical memory probes

Step 5.2 — Neural Activity Validation
  - Compare simulated spike patterns / EEG-analog to pre-preservation recordings
  - Statistical comparison of functional connectivity
  - Sleep/wake cycle emergence: does it spontaneously sleep?

Step 5.3 — Ablation Testing
  - Remove specific simulated regions → compare to known lesion syndromes
  - If simulated hippocampus lesion → anterograde amnesia → structural fidelity confirmed

Step 5.4 — Self-Report (if subjective experience present)
  - Ask the emulation: do you have memories of being the original person?
  - This is epistemically limited — a well-structured zombie could give same answers

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
ESTIMATED TIMELINE (as of 2024, best-case scenario)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

  2025-2030:  Full mouse brain micro-connectome (complete)
              First-generation real-time mouse brain mesoscale simulation
              Neuropixels-scale chronic recording in humans (BCI advances)

  2030-2040:  Human brain macro+meso connectome (MRI + optical)
              First simplified WBE attempts (functional fidelity level 1-2)
              Neuromorphic hardware at mouse-brain-equivalent scale

  2040-2050:  First full human microconnectome (from donated postmortem)
              Level 3 (LIF) human brain simulation (much slower than real-time)
              Neuromorphic scale reaches ~1% of human brain real-time

  2050-2070:  Real-time human brain simulation at LIF level (neuromorphic)
              First serious WBE experiments; major philosophical/ethical debate
              Non-destructive imaging still a barrier (~nm resolution in vivo)

  2070-2100:  Speculative: real-time HH-level simulation; non-destructive
              uploading approaches (if molecular nanotechnology advances)

  NOTE: These projections have historically been optimistic in neuroscience.
  The brain is far more complex than any single technology roadmap can capture.

APPENDIX C: GLOSSARY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

  Action Potential:    All-or-nothing electrical spike; neuron's binary output
  Afferent:            Carrying signals toward the brain/CNS
  AMPA receptor:       Fast glutamate receptor; Na⁺ permeable; mediates EPSP
  Axon:                Long process of neuron; transmits signals to next cell
  BDNF:                Brain-derived neurotrophic factor; growth + plasticity
  BBB:                 Blood-Brain Barrier; protects brain from bloodstream
  Brodmann Area:       Anatomical classification of cortical regions by cell type
  Connectome:          Complete map of neural connections in a nervous system
  Dendrite:            Branched input receiver of a neuron
  DMN:                 Default Mode Network; active at rest; self-referential
  EPSP:                Excitatory Post-Synaptic Potential; depolarizes neuron
  Efferent:            Carrying signals away from brain/CNS to body
  GABA:                γ-aminobutyric acid; main inhibitory neurotransmitter
  Glial cell:          Non-neuronal brain cell; support, modulation, immunity
  Glutamate:           Main excitatory neurotransmitter
  HH model:            Hodgkin-Huxley; most accurate computational neuron model
  Homunculus:          Distorted body map in motor/sensory cortex
  IPSP:                Inhibitory Post-Synaptic Potential; hyperpolarizes neuron
  LIF:                 Leaky Integrate-and-Fire; simple spiking neuron model
  LTP:                 Long-Term Potentiation; synaptic strengthening; memory
  LTD:                 Long-Term Depression; synaptic weakening; selectivity
  Meninges:            Three membranes surrounding the brain
  Myelination:         Fatty insulation around axons; speeds conduction
  NMDA receptor:       Slow glutamate receptor; Ca²⁺ permeable; LTP gate
  Neurogenesis:        Birth of new neurons (adult: DG of hippocampus)
  Neuroplasticity:     Brain's ability to change with experience
  Neurotransmitter:    Chemical released at synapse to signal next neuron
  Qualia:              Subjective qualities of experience; "what it's like"
  RPE:                 Reward Prediction Error; encoded by dopamine neurons
  Refractory period:   Time after spike during which neuron cannot re-fire
  Saltatory conduction: Spike "jumping" between nodes of Ranvier in myelinated axon
  Synapse:             Junction between two neurons; site of communication
  Thalamus:            Sensory relay; gatekeeper to cortex
  WBE:                 Whole Brain Emulation; full computational copy of a brain
